Sample 1 · Course review

An accessibility review of my own online course

I ran the review on my own course before offering it to anyone else. The audit reads the course export file, the file Canvas produces under Settings, Export Course Content, which carries course content and no student records. It checks every page, assignment, quiz and the syllabus against WCAG 2.1 AA with fixed rules, so the same course produces the same list on every run.

Every finding gives the item, its location in the course, a severity and a specific fix. Severity follows what can be proved from the page code: an error is a failure the markup confirms, a warning is very likely a failure with a legitimate exception, and a note needs a person to look.

Error1.1.1

Welcome Page: image alt text is the file name

wiki_content/welcome-page.html · alt="Full Body.jpg"

A screen reader announces "Full Body.jpg," which describes nothing.

FixWrite alt text that says what the photo shows and why it is on the page, or mark it decorative.

Error1.3.1

Syllabus: grading table has no header cells

course_settings/syllabus.html · table "Grading Element / Weight / Description"

The header row is built from bold text in ordinary cells, so every cell is read out with no column label.

FixMake the first row real header cells and add a caption that names the table.

Warning1.4.3

The NFL and Television: section headings too faint

wiki_content/the-nfl-and-television.html · color #3498db on white

Contrast ratio 3.15:1, below the 4.5:1 minimum for body-size text.

FixDarken the heading color until it clears 4.5:1, then recheck the page.

Note3.2.5

Syllabus: a link opens a new tab without saying so

course_settings/syllabus.html · instructor email link, target="_blank"

Markup can't settle this one. A person decides whether the new tab is needed.

FixSay so in the link text, or let the link open in the same tab.

Four of 62 findings shown. The full report lists every finding by item, with the code that triggered it.

Sample 2 · Assignment design

Insider Gambling: The Red Team

A scenario assignment for sports media students, 250 points, taught at the University of Colorado Boulder (CMDI 2001, Fall 2026) and at Green River College (JOURN 156). Panels take the position of someone inside a sport who wants to profit from information the public does not have. They build the scheme, find where it breaks, and then switch sides and write the reporting that would catch them.

Part 1 · Build

The scheme

Which sports are easiest to corrupt? Who in the building knows something first, and how do they get paid without their name on it? Prop bets let someone move one player's stat line and leave the score alone. Does that count as fixing?

Part 2 · Break

Where it falls apart

The case is Alabama baseball, 2023. Suspicion started with a very large bet, placed in person, on a game nobody cared about. Students work out why monitoring is tightest where games matter least, and which workarounds still leave a trace.

Part 3 · Switch sides

The reporting plan

Now the panel is the reporter. Which records would they pull, from whom? Which sources would they work, and what would those sources risk by talking? What does the pattern look like in the betting line before anyone admits anything?

How it is scaffolded

Sep 28Warm-up discussion Oct 5Workshop Oct 7The Exchange Oct 9Written brief

The warm-up discussion gives three people at the same championship game, the Fixer, the Spot-Rigger and the Intel Operator. Students rank them and say exactly where the line gets crossed. The ranking becomes the vocabulary for the scheme.

The Exchange was built as a live session in which every panel holds a station at once while classmates circulate and try to knock holes in the scheme. Most panels presented by recorded video instead. The written brief carried the same three parts either way, so the assessment held.

Design choices

  • The role switch carries the learning. Building the scheme makes the reporting plan concrete.
  • A single named case anchors an open-ended problem.
  • The guardrail is written into the prompt: an analytical exercise in how integrity fails and how it gets caught, with no accusations against real people beyond adjudicated cases.
  • The brief is the record of the work, not a separate paper.
This is the half most groups underbuild. The assignment sheet, on Part 3
Sample 3 · Rubric alignment and revision

JOURN 101 Final Multimedia News Story: fixing the directions, not the rubric

Two students in a fully online JOURN 101 section ran into the same confusion over how to format and submit the final project. I wrote the assignment and its rubric, so I checked the two against each other. Every rubric criterion was already covered by a stated requirement. The problem was delivery: the directions offered a live-website option that caused publishing and visibility trouble, and they never said what file to hand in.

I rewrote the directions. There is now one required format, a PDF export, with a one-line export path for each free tool students use. The live-website option is gone, and the hard-coded due date became "at the Canvas deadline." The design work and the rubric stayed the same. That version now runs every term.

Sample 4 · Asynchronous course structure

A week in my online courses

Most of my online students work, and many log in after a shift. I design for the student who opens the course at 11 p.m. with nobody to ask. The weekly rhythm is fixed so students can plan around jobs, and the full term calendar is posted on the first day.

MonModule opens: overview page lists readings, work due and deadlines
TueReading quiz, two attempts
WedReporting or production work
ThuDiscussion post due
FriVideo office hours by appointment
SatTwo replies to classmates due
SunReplies to students within one to two business days, all week

Discussion rubric, 25 points

Five criteria at five points each: answering every part of the question; quality of the response and its use of course concepts; mechanics; replies that add a new idea instead of agreeing; and posting one initial response and two replies on time and at length. The lowest quiz and discussion scores drop, so one bad week does not sink a grade.

Engagement that holds up online

  • Prompts ask for a position backed by a source, and replies have to build on, question or answer a classmate.
  • JOURN 156 opens with an introduction video and virtual team building.
  • Its first major project is a debate segment in the format of ESPN's Around the Horn, produced by remote teams and then peer-reviewed.
  • Reporting assignments send students out to real events and real interviews, so the course does not stay on the screen.
Sample 5 · Build and quality tools

A course system I designed and directed

I designed the system and decided what each tool had to do and check. The code was written with AI-assisted coding: Python against the Canvas LMS API. It has run my Canvas courses at the Colorado School of Mines, CU Boulder and Green River College since April 2026, across 347 recorded changes. Each tool exists because a course needed it.

Build

Course from a syllabus

Builds a whole course shell from a syllabus and a term calendar: one module per teaching week, each with an overview page and that week's graded items, with every due date computed from the new term. Everything is created unpublished for review, and a rerun skips anything already there.

Build

Scheduler, calendar and course copy

Sets a term's deadlines from the term dates, builds module overview pages and an accessible course calendar from live due dates, copies a course into a new term with the dates shifted, and builds Canvas rubrics.

Before the term opens

Launch checklist

Asks whether a course is ready to open to students. It flags due dates outside the term and weighted grade groups with nothing in them. It reads only, changes nothing, and links to the tool that fixes each kind of problem.

Every day, built November 2026

Look-ahead check

A Green River quiz asked about two videos and a scenario the course never showed. The quiz description that listed them had been lost in a course copy, and nobody knew until a student wrote in. The check now reads next week's quizzes and assignments against what the course actually shows, and it flags links to pages that do not exist.

Any time

Accessibility audit and fixer

The rule-based WCAG 2.1 AA audit in Sample 1. It works on a live course or on an export file, and a fixer repairs headings, tables and links on assignment pages.

Audit of the tool itself

Checking the checker

I audit the software the way I audit a course. A June 2026 review found that deadlines set in UTC were closing Green River assignments at 4:59 p.m. Pacific. The scheduler now works in each course's local time.

Sample 6 · Multimedia

Live class recordings into captioned teaching video

I produced audio for twenty years and have released albums of my own. In November 2026 I built a pipeline that turns a class recorded on a phone into a finished, captioned video matched to the slides, with the clips shown in the room. Captions also count toward accessibility.

Phone or Zoom recording→Word-level transcript→Cuts on exact words→Slides and clips matched→Burned-in captions
  • Cuts land on word boundaries, never on rough timestamps, and every seam is reviewed before export.
  • Each clip starts at its setup question, so a viewer always has the context.
  • Student voices are never included. Student answers appear as on-screen cards marked as paraphrase.
  • Every segment is labeled on screen with course, school, date and section.
How I use AI in design work

AI drafts, and I own what reaches a learner

A structured workflow

I use Claude every day, including Claude Code, and I write Claude skills: packaged, reusable instructions for tasks I repeat. AI handles source analysis, drafting, format conversion and first-pass checks. I set the objective, the constraints and the examples.

Checked before it ships

Every AI draft gets read against the learning objective and the source before a student sees it. Quotation marks go only around words found in the source, and every figure says what it counts.

Fixed rules where consistency matters

A model's opinion changes from run to run, and a compliance check can't. That is why the accessibility audit runs on fixed rules. AI is used where judgment helps and kept out where a repeatable answer is the point.

Ask first, then rule

My syllabi carry no fixed AI policy. Students ask before they use a tool, I rule on it, and the rulings build into precedent over the term. A policy written in August is out of date by October.
